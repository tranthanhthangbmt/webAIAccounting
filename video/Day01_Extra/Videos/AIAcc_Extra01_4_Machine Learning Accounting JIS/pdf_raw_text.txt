ABSTRACT: Machine learning (ML) is increasingly used to detect fraud, predict distress, analyze disclosures, and 
support audit work. Prior reviews map applications or methods but rarely connect accounting problems to ML design, 
validation, and governance choices. We reviewed 273 peer-reviewed Web of Science Core Collection articles 
published from 1992 to 2025, combining bibliometric science mapping with an antecedents-decisions-outcomes 
(ADO) synthesis enriched by a theory-context-method (TCM) lens. The literature clusters around distress and 
bankruptcy prediction, reporting quality, and fraud, bridged by audit analytics and textual analysis. ML adoption is 
driven mainly by assurance pressure, unstructured information, and digital process data; however, many studies rely 
on accuracy-oriented metrics, nontemporal validation, and explainability that is reported rather than tested. We show 
how accounting conditions should discipline ML evaluation and propose a research agenda for cost-aware, 
temporally robust, and governable ML in accounting.
Data Availability: Data are available on request from the authors.
Keywords: machine learning; accounting; auditing; bibliometric analysis; ADO-TCM framework.

Simultaneously, accounting settings impose constraints that complicate the direct translation of predictive 
performance into decision value. ML outputs often support professional judgment under asymmetric error costs, tem­
poral dependence, and accountability requirements that emphasize documentation, reproducibility, and reviewability, 
especially when models inform audit planning, exception triage, or reporting quality assessments (Bertomeu 2020;

disclosures and process-level data (e.g., deep learning for restatement classification, natural language processing 
(NLP)-based interpretation of key audit matters, process mining combined with deep learning for suspicious transac­
tion detection) (Hayes and Boritz 2021; Liu, Yen, and Wu 2022; Wang, T. Chiu, V. Chiu, and Vasarhelyi 2025).
As the literature has expanded, synthesizing what we know has become increasingly difficult. Prior reviews often orga­
nize evidence either by topical domains (e.g., fraud detection, distress prediction, and textual analysis) or by families of 
methods (e.g., neural networks, ensembles, and NLP), which is informative but can fragment the accumulation of knowl­
edge (Amani and Fadlalla 2017; Kureljusic and Karger 2024). Topic- or method-centric syntheses are useful, but they often 
leave one question underdeveloped: whether ML design and validation choices fit the accounting problem being addressed. 
This matters because fraud detection, distress prediction, disclosure analytics, and audit planning impose different require­
ments on error costs, validation, documentation, and reviewability (Bertomeu 2020; Hardies, Ohlrogge, Mentens, and 
Vandennieuwenhuysen 2024). Without a linkage-based synthesis, there is a risk of conflating “algorithmic accuracy” with 
accounting value and of overlooking the governance conditions required for models to be auditable and useful in practice.
Two limitations in existing reviews motivate our study. First, prior syntheses clarify where ML is used in accounting, but 
they provide less evidence on whether ML design choices fit the accounting problems they address. This distinction matters 
because the same performance metric or validation design does not carry the same meaning across fraud detection, distress 
prediction, disclosure analytics, and audit planning. Fraud and misstatement settings involve asymmetric error costs; distress 
and disclosure settings often involve temporal dependence; and audit settings require documentation and reviewability. A 
review of this literature, therefore, needs to connect the accounting conditions to the ML decision and the claimed outcome. 
Second, theory is often used to motivate the empirical setting but less often to discipline ML design and evaluation choices, 
such as cost-sensitive loss functions, validation windows, threshold selection, or explainability artifacts.
To address this gap, we combine an antecedents-decisions-outcomes (ADO) synthesis with a theory-context- 
method (TCM) lens (Paul, Parthasarathy, and Gupta 2017; Paul and Benito 2018). ADO is needed because it links the 
accounting conditions that motivate ML adoption to the design and validation decisions through which ML is imple­
mented and to the outcomes that are claimed. TCM is needed because it shows how this evidence is produced: which 
theories frame the problem, which accounting contexts are examined, and which methods support the claims reported. 
Used together, ADO and TCM allow us to move beyond a descriptive inventory of applications and toward a synthesis 
of whether the ML choices are aligned with accounting problems.
This framing leads directly to two research questions. The first maps the structure of the field and how evidence is 
produced, whereas the second examines whether accounting antecedents are matched by appropriate ML design, 
validation, and outcome claims. Accordingly, we ask:
RQ1: What intellectual structure, application domains, and theory-context-method patterns characterize 
ML research in accounting?
RQ2: How do accounting antecedents shape ML design and validation decisions, and how do these decisions 
condition reported outcomes and future research gaps?
We answer RQ1 through bibliometric science mapping and TCM coding. The bibliometric analysis identifies the field’s 
thematic structure and research fronts, whereas the theory, context, and method codes show how evidence is produced across 
accounting settings. We answer RQ2 through the ADO synthesis, which links adoption antecedents to design and validation 
decisions and the outcomes those decisions condition. This sequencing ensures that the descriptive map of the field is used as 
the basis for a more diagnostic assessment of alignment between accounting problems and ML choices.

II. BACKGROUND AND POSITIONING
Machine learning (ML) has moved from early classification tools for distress and fraud to a set of deployable 
analytics supporting accounting and assurance tasks where signals are high-dimensional, heterogeneous, and often 
unstructured. Foundational studies applied neural networks to financial distress and fraud risk (Coats and Fant 1993; 
Yang, M. Platt, and H. Platt 1999), whereas more recent work demonstrates how modern ML pipelines improve 
screening and prioritization for misstatements and fraud under rare events and asymmetric costs (Perols 2011; 
Bertomeu 2020; Parker, Jiang, Cho, and Vasarhelyi 2025; Bao et al. 2020). In Accounting Information Systems (AIS) 
outlets, the stream has broadened to disclosure and audit-text analytics and process-aware audit applications, including 
restatement classification and sentiment extraction from audit communications, and deep-learning process mining for 
suspicious transaction detection (Hayes and Boritz 2021; Duan, Vasarhelyi, and Codesso 2025; Wang et al. 2025).
Existing reviews document this expansion, but they usually organize the evidence by application domains or 
method families. This is useful for mapping the field, yet it makes the fit between accounting conditions and ML 
choices less visible. The key unresolved issue is therefore not whether ML is used in accounting, but whether design, 
validation, and explanation practices are aligned with the decision setting in which the model is expected to operate.
Accordingly, our review complements prior work by combining bibliometric science mapping with an antecedents- 
decisions-outcomes synthesis enriched by a theory-context-method lens (Paul et al. 2017; Paul and Benito 2018). 
Bibliometrics identifies the field’s structure, whereas ADO-TCM connects adoption drivers, design and validation 
choices, reported outcomes, and the evidentiary base supporting them. This positioning shifts the review from a 
descriptive catalog of applications toward a diagnostic assessment of whether ML methods are appropriate for the 
accounting problems they address.
In this synthesis, ADO is used to link accounting antecedents, ML decisions, and reported outcomes rather than 
merely to classify papers. Antecedents include conditions such as fraud risk, assurance pressure, unstructured informa­
tion, and process digitalization. Decisions capture task framing, model design, validation, thresholding, and explain­
ability choices. Outcomes refer to predictive lift, audit-effort allocation, exception triage, and reviewability. This 
structure makes it possible to assess alignment: for instance, fraud and misstatement settings imply asymmetric error 
costs, so evidence based only on accuracy or area under the curve (AUC) provides a weaker account of accounting 
decision value than evidence using cost-sensitive evaluation.
TCM plays a complementary role by documenting how the evidence is produced: which theories frame the prob­
lem, which accounting contexts are examined, and which methods support the claims. Together, ADO and TCM allow 
us to identify dominant applications as well as mismatches, such as nontemporal validation in time-dependent settings 
or explainability artifacts reported without evidence of reviewer use.

To strengthen transparency, we specified the protocol ex ante and reported study selection through a PRISMA-

The methodology proceeds in four stages: corpus definition and screening; search-string and eligibility specifica­
tion; bibliometric mapping of the field’s intellectual and thematic structure; and article-level ADO-TCM coding for the 
framework-based synthesis.
Data Collection
We assembled the dataset from the WoSCC, using a single source to ensure replicability, stable metadata, and a 
controlled peer-reviewed journal corpus. This choice is appropriate for bibliometric mapping because co-citation, 
keyword, source, and country analyses are sensitive to duplicates, inconsistent indexing, and heterogeneous metadata 
across databases. WoSCC also supports transparent filtering by document type, subject category, language, and index 
and allows consistent export into Bibliometrix/Biblioshiny (Aria and Cuccurullo 2017). To capture relevant accounting 
information systems research, we included both the Social Sciences Citation Index (SSCI) and the Emerging Sources 
Citation Index (ESCI) and retained the WoS categories Business, Business Finance, and Management (De Filippo and 
Gorraiz 2020; Pranckut_e 2021; Martín-Martín, Orduna-Malea, Thelwall, and Delgado Lopez-Cozar 2018). We also 
checked the corpus against key accounting and AIS outlets, including the International Journal of Accounting 
Information Systems, the Journal of Emerging Technologies in Accounting, the Journal of Information Systems, and 
major accounting journals such as The Accounting Review, Contemporary Accounting Research, Accounting Horizons, 
and the Journal of Accounting Research. We therefore treat WoSCC as a high-quality and reproducible corpus for 
mapping peer-reviewed ML accounting research, while recognizing that it does not cover the full interdisciplinary 
literature indexed elsewhere.
Procedure
The search strategy was designed to identify machine learning studies with an explicit accounting, auditing, or 
accountability focus. We searched WoS Topic fields, which include title, abstract, author keywords, and Keywords 
Plus, using the following query:
TS¼ ((“machine learning“ OR “deep learning” OR “supervised learning” OR “unsupervised learning” OR 
“reinforcement learning” OR “neural network” OR “random forest” OR “support vector” OR “gradient 
boosting” OR “xgboost” OR “forecast” OR “predict”) AND (“accounting” OR “auditing” OR 
“accountability” OR “accountant” OR “auditor”))
The search was run in January 2026, but the analytical window was closed on December 31, 2025. We excluded 
2026 records to avoid partial-year bias in annual production and trend analyses.
The initial query returned 87,594 records. We then applied sequential filters: publication years excluding 
2026 (n ¼ 87,054); Articles and Early Access only (n ¼ 76,600); WoS subject categories Business, Business Finance, and 
Management (n ¼ 4,993); SSCI and ESCI indices (n ¼ 3,573 + 1,311 ¼ 4,884); and English language only (n ¼ 4,855).
Two authors independently screened titles and abstracts against the review scope: ML applied to accounting, 
auditing, accountability problems, constructs, or settings. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, when 
needed, third-reader arbitration. Records retained after title and abstract screening underwent full-text eligibility 
checks. At this stage, we excluded studies using ML solely for nonaccounting financial-market prediction, nonscholarly 
sources, and papers that did not critically assess ML methods. Screening decisions were logged record by record in a 
structured spreadsheet using standardized decision codes and exclusion rationales. The final analytical sample includes 
273 papers for bibliometric and framework-based qualitative synthesis. The broad initial query, including terms such 
as “forecast” and “predict,” was intentionally inclusive to avoid missing early or methodologically diverse studies 
that did not use the label “machine learning.” Online Appendix Table A1 reports the exclusion rationales applied 
during screening.

Bibliometric Analysis
For the bibliometric strand, we used Bibliometrix/Biblioshiny (Aria and Cuccurullo 2017) to extract and analyze standard 
fields (sources, authors, citations, countries, keywords) and to produce science-mapping outputs (e.g., co-citation networks to 
identify intellectual foundations; and keyword co-occurrence maps to delineate thematic clusters). Parameter settings and 
thresholds (e.g., minimum co-occurrence frequency) were held constant across analyses to preserve comparability; detailed 
specifications are provided in the Online Appendix. In addition, we used the “Biblio AI” extension, powered by Google 
Gemini (Gemini 2.5 Flash model), only as an auxiliary tool for initial keyword-cluster interpretation and contextual summari­
zation. Final topic labels and trend groups were not taken directly from the AI output. They were manually reviewed, harmo­
nized, and aligned with prior accounting and ML review categories, distinguishing substantive accounting domains from 
generic field descriptors and method labels. The bibliometric analysis provides the field-level map used to answer RQ1 and to 
identify the thematic clusters examined more diagnostically in the subsequent ADO-TCM synthesis.
Framework-Based Coding
For the qualitative strand, we used an ADO-TCM coding scheme. For each article, we coded antecedents, deci­
sions, and outcomes, and recorded its theory, context, and method to characterize how the evidence was produced. The 
ADO fields captured adoption drivers, design and validation choices, and reported outcomes; the TCM fields captured 
theoretical lens, empirical setting, data source, methodological design, ML task, validation protocol, and use of 
explainable AI (XAI). Two researchers independently coded the articles using a shared codebook. Initial agreement 
was 91.6 percent across 1,638 article-level ADO-TCM coding decisions,1 with 138 disagreements. Disagreements were 
resolved through discussion; 17 cases (1.0 percent) required third-reader arbitration before reaching consensus.
The move from coding to synthesis followed five steps. First, each article was assigned article-level ADO codes to identify 
the accounting antecedent, the ML design or validation decision, and the reported outcome. Second, each article was coded 
for TCM to record the theoretical framing, accounting context, and methodological design supporting the evidence. Third, 
after coder reconciliation, we constructed a consensus coding matrix and grouped repeated ADO combinations within each 
context. Fourth, we assessed alignment between antecedents and decisions; for example, we assessed whether fraud and mis­
statement studies used cost-sensitive metrics, whether time-dependent accounting tasks used temporal validation, and whether 
explainability artifacts supported reviewer judgment rather than merely describing model outputs. Fifth, we aggregated these 
patterns into the synthesis reported in Section V, distinguishing recurring alignments, misalignments, and future research gaps.
The ADO structure supports a cumulative narrative that links why ML is adopted in accounting (A), how research­
ers and practitioners design and validate ML systems (D), and what theoretical and practical impacts ensue (O). This 
framework-based synthesis is consistent with prior knowledge-mapping work that embeds ADO within hybrid reviews to 
identify robust gaps and future research pathways and aligns with recent accounting SLR guidance (Massaro et al. 2016). 
The resulting ADO-TCM matrix provides the bridge between the descriptive bibliometric results in Section IV and the 
diagnostic synthesis in Section V.
The complete article-level coding matrix, including year, authors, source, DOI, theoretical lens, and methodologi­
cal classification, is reported in Online Appendix Table A2.

The bibliometric analysis maps the corpus’s growth, venues, author structure, influential contributions, thematic 
evolution, conceptual clusters, and collaboration geography. These results answer the mapping component of RQ1 and 
provide the field-level structure interpreted through ADO-TCM coding in Section V.

Citations Analysis
Table 4 shows that the most cited contributions combine predictive applications with governance and professional 
concerns. Influential work includes ML-based fraud and misstatement detection (Bao et al. 2020; Bertomeu, Cheynel, 
Floyd, and Pan 2021), ethical implications of AI-enabled auditing (Munoko, Brown-Liburd, and Vasarhelyi 2020), 
human-algorithm collaboration (Grønsund and Aanestad 2020), and early neural-network applications to distress and 
fraud risk (Coats and Fant 1993; Green and Choi 1997; Yang et al. 1999). This citation structure shows that the field’s 
influential work already links prediction with usability, defensibility, and governance in accounting settings.
Trend Topics
Figure 4 reports thematic evolution using harmonized article-level topic groups rather than raw keyword labels. 
We excluded generic descriptors such as “accounting,” “machine learning,” “artificial intelligence,” and “algorithm(s),” 
and merged overlapping method labels such as “neural network,” “artificial neural network,” and “deep learning.” The 
resulting groups distinguish substantive domains: distress and going-concern prediction; fraud, misstatement, and report­
ing quality; audit analytics and assurance; textual disclosure and NLP; earnings management and governance; AIS, pro­
cess mining, and continuous monitoring; and management accounting, education, and adoption.
The trend analysis shows a shift from early distress, bankruptcy, and neural-network forecasting work toward 
fraud, reporting quality, audit analytics, and textual disclosure. Since 2020, the field has become more explicitly con­
nected to assurance and reporting problems, especially through unstructured disclosures and process-level data. These 
domains anchor the ADO-TCM synthesis in Section V.

Figure 5 maps the field through an all-keywords co-occurrence network. “Machine learning” is the central hub, 
with “artificial intelligence” as a secondary hub. The main communities capture AI-enabled accounting and auditing 
systems, predictive modeling and risk assessment, reporting outcomes and governance mechanisms, and disclosure/tex­
tual analysis. The structure is modular but interconnected through auditing, risk, and prediction, showing the diffusion 
of ML across assurance, distress prediction, reporting quality, and disclosure analytics.

V. RESULTS: ADO-TCM SYNTHESIS
Building on the bibliometric mapping, we now trace how accounting conditions drive ML adoption and shape 
methodological choices. The bibliometric analysis identified the main application domains and thematic clusters; the 
ADO-TCM synthesis explains how those domains operate at the article level. The ADO coding identifies the account­
ing antecedents associated with each study, the ML design and validation decisions used in response, and the outcomes 
claimed. The TCM coding records the theoretical lenses, empirical contexts, and methods through which this evidence 
is produced. The purpose of this section is therefore to move from mapping the field to explaining the links between 
accounting problems, ML choices, and reported value.

Antecedents (A): Why is Machine Learning Adopted in Accounting?
Across the 273 reviewed studies, four antecedent clusters drive ML adoption in accounting. Each cluster exerts a 
distinct pressure on the design choices analyzed in the “Decisions (D): How Is ML Designed and Validated in 
Accounting?” subsection, and identifying these conditional pathways is necessary to move beyond a descriptive taxon­
omy of “drivers.” The synthesis presented here specifies which antecedent conditions tend to elicit which methodologi­
cal responses, and why these alignments matter for the outcomes reported in the literature.
The first cluster concerns assurance and compliance pressures, which dominate the audit subset of the corpus 
(113 studies, 41.4 percent of the sample). When risk indicators are numerous, heterogeneous, and time-varying, ML is 
mobilized to operationalize complex risk profiles and improve the ex ante targeting of audit effort (Parker et al. 2025; 
Wang et al. 2025; Wu and Dull 2025). This antecedent generates an asymmetric error-cost structure, since false nega­
tives in fraud detection carry materially higher costs than false positives. In principle, this should push design toward 
cost-sensitive evaluation, but the empirical pattern indicates otherwise. Although 21 percent of fraud-focused studies 
adopt rare-event handling techniques such as synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) or class weighting, 
only 3 percent of audit-context studies and none of the fraud-context studies adopt explicitly cost-sensitive evaluation 
metrics. Recognition of data scarcity has not yet translated into evaluations aligned with decision costs, a failure we 
examine in detail in the decisions subsection. This first cluster, therefore, shows how an accounting condition, assur­
ance pressure, creates a methodological requirement that is only partially reflected in current ML evaluation practices.
The second cluster reflects the growth of high-dimensional unstructured information. It emerges as the dominant 
antecedent in 30 studies on narrative disclosure and audit-text analytics, and it appears pervasively across the 273 stud­
ies that incorporate textual or process-trace data. Expanded audit reporting (Key Audit Matters, Critical Audit 
Matters), MD&A narratives, and process-log artifacts produce signals that traditional analytic procedures cannot con­
sistently extract (Fulcer et al. 2025; Xie and Li 2026; Hayes and Boritz 2021). This antecedent imposes two specific 
design pressures. First, it requires representation-learning pipelines such as transfer learning or embeddings. Second, it 
makes auditability difficult to preserve when intermediate features are not human-interpretable. For example, NLP- 
based disclosure studies can extract incremental information from narrative text, but this benefit creates an accompany­
ing need to document how textual features are represented and how model outputs can be reviewed. The empirical 
record shows that explainability artifacts remain underadopted in this stream (3 percent in the text/disclosure subset, 
against 12 percent in distress prediction), suggesting that the contexts most reliant on opaque representations are also

The third cluster centers on early-warning prediction under nonlinear and opaque accounting signals. It character­

Lin, Padliansyah, Lu, and Liu 2025; Lee and Tahmoush 2025). This antecedent has been recognized longest in the liter­
ature, dating to the foundational work of Coats and Fant (1993) and Yang et al. (1999). Its temporal nature, which 
involves predicting future states from current accounting data, imposes a clear design requirement: validation must 
reflect the time-ordered structure of the information flow. This antecedent leads to design decisions around out-of-time 
validation, rolling windows, and robustness to changes in economic regimes. The empirical pattern is informative. 
Distress studies show the highest temporal-validation rate in the corpus (12 percent against 1 percent overall), reflecting 
a partial but uneven recognition that out-of-time generalization is the substantive criterion of interest. Even so, 88 per­
cent of distress studies still rely on cross-sectional or nontemporal validation, despite the regime-shift sensitivity well 
documented in the corporate-failure literature. This cluster therefore shows that, even in the most mature predictive 
stream, validation choices remain unevenly aligned with the accounting problem.
The fourth cluster involves process digitalization, continuous-control infrastructures, and accountability or 
responsible-AI concerns (Xiong, Peng, Xiao, and Yang 2025; Otero and Agu 2025; Ashraf 2025; Munoko et al. 2020). 
This antecedent is qualitatively different from the previous three because it shifts the focal mechanism from “ML as 
predictor” to “ML as organizational artifact requiring governance.” It motivates documentation, audit-trail design, 
and human-algorithm review protocols. For example, process-level audit applications depend not only on whether the 
model flags suspicious transactions but also on whether the model’s inputs, transformations, thresholds, and reviewer 
actions can be reconstructed after the fact. As the Outcomes subsection develops, this is also the antecedent most conse­
quential for whether reported predictive gains translate into durable assurance value, since governance shortfalls are 
repeatedly identified as the binding constraint on deployment. The four clusters are neither mutually exclusive nor uni­
formly distributed across the corpus. Their substantive relevance lies in what they predict. Each antecedent generates a 
specific design pressure that, in turn, conditions which design choices are theoretically appropriate and which boundary 
conditions later govern the realization of outcomes. Therefore, the antecedents show that ML adoption in accounting 
is not driven solely by technology but by accounting conditions that impose distinct evaluation, validation, and gover­
nance requirements. The next subsection explicitly develops these antecedent-decision linkages.
Decisions (D): How is ML Designed and Validated in Accounting?
Design decisions in the corpus are best understood as conditional responses to the antecedent pressures identified 
above, rather than as a flat enumeration of methodological choices. Three analytical dimensions structure this synthe­
sis. The first concerns how task framing and evaluation respond to asymmetric error costs. The second concerns how 
validation design responds to the temporal structure of accounting evidence. The third concerns how explainability 
functions, or fails to function, as an accountability mechanism rather than a descriptive feature. In each case, the rele­
vant question is whether the ML decision matches the accounting condition that motivated adoption.
First, task framing and evaluation often fail to reflect asymmetric error costs. Across the corpus, 112 studies (41.0 
percent) report performance using accuracy, F1, AUC, precision, or recall as primary criteria. Only 28 studies (10.3 
percent) adopt cost-sensitive evaluation or expected-cost frameworks in which false-negative and false-positive errors 
carry differential weights aligned with audit or assurance objectives. The disproportion becomes striking when 
juxtaposed with the antecedent structure. Fraud and misstatement contexts (33 studies) display the highest rate of 
accuracy-only reporting (36 percent), even though these are settings in which the cost of missing a true fraud is materi­
ally asymmetric to the cost of a false alarm. Three illustrative cases stand out among the studies that explicitly engage 
with this asymmetry.
Wu and Dull (2025) frame misclassification costs explicitly in the nonprofit discontinuance setting, motivated by 
“donors’ asymmetric losses from false negatives versus false positives.” Tragouda, Doumpos, and Zopounidis (2024)
implement cost-sensitive weighting for class imbalance with rolling temporal validation in financial-statement fraud, 
combining both substantive design responses to the antecedent structure.
Huang and Wang (2023) prioritize recall, precision, and misclassification cost over accuracy in an audit-pedagogy set­
ting and explicitly frame model selection as a professional judgment. These cases stand in contrast to a majority of fraud 
and audit studies that report area under the curve as the headline metric, even when threshold selection is left implicit. 
The implication for cumulative knowledge is direct: when evaluation does not embed the cost structure imposed by the 
antecedent, reported performance is decoupled from the decision value the model is intended to produce. This decoupling 
offers a structural explanation for why claims of “predictive lift” tend to attenuate when reinterpreted for assurance use.

Thus, the first decision pattern links assurance and fraud antecedents to a recurring methodological gap: evaluation met­
rics often assess classification performance without accounting for accounting decision value.
Second, the validation design is unevenly aligned with the temporal structure of accounting evidence. The corpus 
shows a comparable disconnect between antecedent and design. Only 35 studies (12.8 percent) explicitly employ blocked, 
out-of-time, or rolling-window validation, and 21 studies (7.7 percent) explicitly engage with class imbalance and rare- 
event modeling. Joint adoption is rarer still. Audit and assurance studies, for which fiscal-year boundaries, regime shifts, 
and standard cycles create strong temporal dependence, show only a 2 percent adoption rate for temporal validation. The 
distress and bankruptcy subset, by contrast, shows 12 percent adoption. Distress prediction has historically internalized 
the temporal logic of the prediction problem, and the study by Serrano-Cinca, Gutierrez-Nieto, and Bernate-Valbuena 
(2019) illustrates the canonical practice, namely pairing failed and healthy firms by industry and size for training and then 
validating out-of-time on a later-period holdout. Audit and assurance studies more frequently rely on stratified random 
splits, which can lead to data leakage when an engagement-year is shared across the train and test partitions. The empiri­
cal pattern thus reveals an asymmetry. Temporal-validation rigor is highest in the antecedent context where its absence 
has been most repeatedly criticized in the literature (Bertomeu 2020) but remains lowest in the antecedent contexts (audit, 
fraud, text and disclosure) where claim defensibility most depends on robust out-of-sample evidence. This second decision 
pattern links temporal accounting problems to a validation gap: many studies test predictive performance as if accounting 
observations were exchangeable, even when the task is explicitly time ordered.
Third, explainability is more often reported than evaluated as an accountability mechanism. The corpus contains 
22 studies (8.1 percent) that incorporate explainability practices into the model-design stage and 39 studies (14.3 percent) 
that report explainability artifacts as part of the reported outcome. Treating explainability as a mechanism rather than a 
feature requires asking what work the artifact does in the assurance chain. The relevant question is whether feature attri­
butions, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values, or interpretable surrogates change how a human reviewer evalu­
ates a flagged observation, documents reasoning in working papers, or escalates exceptions. Three patterns from the 
coded evidence are instructive. Zhang et al. (2022) explicitly frame the design problem as “pairing black-box models with 
appropriate XAI techniques for both global tool understanding and engagement-level (local) explanations,” coupling 
time-based splits with under-sampling to address imbalance. This is an integrated treatment of design, validation, and 
accountability. Trinkle and Baldwin (2016) treat the performance-interpretability tradeoff as a foregrounded design 
choice in credit decisions that must be explained to regulators and applicants and illustrate accountability as the binding 
constraint. Schneider and Br€uhl (2023) add interpretability components (feature importance, functional form, interac­
tions) specifically to explain why the model flags fraud, thereby moving explainability from accuracy supplement to 
explanatory artifact. Against these examples, however, the broader corpus is characterized by explainability adopted but 
not evaluated. Feature-importance rankings are reported, but their effect on reviewer decisions, calibration, or reliance is 
rarely tested. This third decision pattern links accountability antecedents to an explainability gap: many studies produce 
explanations, but few test whether those explanations improve human review or documentation.
Three additional design dimensions (data representation, model-family selection, and feature construction) are docu­
mented in the corpus but exhibit lower conditional variance across antecedent contexts and are therefore reported here in 
compressed form. Representation choices increasingly span unstructured data, including NLP and text-mining pipelines 
in 11 studies and transfer-learning embeddings in disclosure-focused work (Siano and Wysocki 2021), and process-level 
data such as deep-learning process mining for suspicious transactions (Duan et al. 2025). Model families remain domi­
nated by regression-centered designs (102 studies, 37.4 percent) and neural architectures (28 studies, 10.3 percent), with a 
recurrent performance-auditability tradeoff in which interpretable baselines are retained when reviewability is salient 
(Krupa and Minutti-Meza 2022). These additional choices further reinforce the same pattern: ML design in accounting is 
valuable when it is selected in response to the task’s information structure, error costs, and accountability requirements.
Three propositions summarize the antecedent-decision linkages developed above.
Proposition 1: When the antecedent generates asymmetric error costs (fraud, audit, distress), cost-sensitive 
evaluation is theoretically appropriate but empirically rare; reliance on accuracy-only metrics 
decouples claimed performance from decision value.
Proposition 2: When the antecedent imposes temporal dependence (assurance cycles, distress windows, 
disclosure regime shifts), blocked or rolling-window validation is appropriate but rare outside 
the distress subset; nontemporal validation inflates apparent generalization.
Proposition 3: When the antecedent demands accountability and reviewability (audit, regulated credit, 
fraud), explainability must operate as a mechanism whose effect on human review is 
documented; the corpus more frequently treats explainability as a reported artifact, without 
evidence of its decision-relevant function.

Outcomes (O): What Machine Learning Delivers in Accounting?
Building on the decision patterns above, the Outcomes analysis examines what ML is reported to deliver when 
accounting antecedents and methodological choices are aligned. Across the reviewed studies, outcomes concentrate 
on three recurring value claims: predictive lift, expanded assurance coverage/efficiency, and governable decision sup­
port, with clearly stated boundary conditions. First, the most consistent outcome is out-of-sample performance 
improvement over traditional statistical baselines in high-stakes accounting tasks characterized by nonlinearities and 
class imbalance. This pattern is evident in fraud and misstatement screening, where ensemble and modern ML pipe­
lines improve identification and prioritization of risky observations (Bao et al. 2020; Bertomeu et al. 2021; Parker 
et al. 2025). Similar gains are reported when unstructured disclosures are incorporated: transfer-learning/NLP 
models extract incremental signals from narrative text, improving classification and prediction beyond structured 
accounting variables alone (Siano and Wysocki 2021; Hayes and Boritz 2021). These outcomes are strongest when 
ML design responds directly to the information problem, such as when nonlinear risk patterns or text-intensive 
evidence are present.
Second, a substantial set of papers frames outcomes as audit execution benefits: broader test coverage, faster excep­
tion triage, and reallocation of effort from routine sampling to targeted review in journal-entry analytics, process moni­
toring, or continuous controls (Wei, Cho, Vasarhelyi, and Te-Wierik 2024; Duan et al. 2025). Third, outcomes 
increasingly include defensibility and transparency through feature attribution and documented validation. Across 
these outcomes, the boundary condition is consistent: predictive gains do not automatically translate into durable 
assurance value when temporal dependence, preprocessing choices, leakage safeguards, model-risk controls, or review­
ability are underspecified (Zhang et al. 2022; Smith and Lamprecht 2024).

risk and governance mechanisms. Second, information and market-interpretation perspectives, mainly signaling the­

Contexts
The context dimension identifies where ML is studied and where the evidence is concentrated. The corpus is 
dominated by audit and assurance settings, especially fraud, misstatement, anomaly detection, and audit-risk appli­
cations. This concentration is consistent with the ADO evidence reported above: assurance contexts give rise to 
rare-event classification problems, asymmetric error costs, and documentation requirements. A second major context 
concerns financial accounting and reporting, including distress prediction, bankruptcy, earnings management, disclo­
sure quality, restatements, and textual reporting analysis. These studies often rely on firm-level archival data and are 
more likely to frame ML as a predictive or classification tool. A smaller but growing stream examines AIS and 
process-level settings, where enterprise resource planning logs, transaction traces, and continuous-monitoring data 
make ML relevant for control testing and process-aware auditing. Managerial accounting, public-sector accounting, 
small and medium enterprises (SMEs), and education remain underrepresented, although recent studies indicate 
growing interest in these areas. Geographically, the evidence is concentrated in North America, Western Europe, and 
East Asia, with thinner coverage of Africa, Central Asia, and parts of Latin America. Overall, the context coding shows 
that the literature is strongest where large archival, textual, or process data are available, whereas contexts requiring field 
access, organizational process evidence, or public accountability data remain underdeveloped. This contextual concentra­
tion matters for interpreting outcomes: ML evidence is most developed where data are accessible and machine-readable, 
but weaker where accounting work depends on organizational processes, professional interaction, and public account­
ability settings that are harder to observe. The next subsection, therefore, examines whether the methods used in the cor­
pus are sufficient to support both predictive and behavioral claims.
Methods
Methodologically, the literature is dominated by empirical quantitative designs, with a clear prevalence of 
regression-based setups and predictive benchmarking, reflecting the field’s emphasis on out-of-sample performance and 
risk-focused inference (Table 6). In our sample, 102 papers use regression-centered empirical designs, often as a baseline 
or as the main analytic approach in fraud/misstatement and distress-related settings. A second, sizeable stream relies 
on neural-network architectures (28 papers), which are typically motivated by nonlinearities and high-dimensional 
inputs and increasingly include unstructured data. Complementing these approaches, NLP/text-mining pipelines 
appear in 11 papers, signaling a shift toward disclosure- and narrative-intensive accounting tasks where representation 
learning provides an incremental signal. Beyond these core empirical traditions, we observe 43 mixed-method contribu­
tions and 32 conceptual papers, whereas qualitative designs remain comparatively rare (case studies: seven; interviews: 
five), typically used to unpack implementation, professional change, and governance mechanisms around ML adop­
tion. Finally, the evidence base includes a limited set of experimental studies (seven), suggesting that causal identifica­
tion of how decision-makers use (or misuse) ML outputs remains underdeveloped relative to predictive modeling work. 
Overall, the methodological profile indicates strong progress on predictive validity, alongside opportunities to 
strengthen explanatory and behavioral evidence through experiments and qualitative inquiry that connect ML outputs 
to accounting judgment and accountability. Taken together, the TCM evidence reinforces the central ADO finding: the 
literature has developed strong evidence on where ML can improve prediction but weaker evidence on how ML should 
be governed, explained, and incorporated into accounting judgment. This conclusion provides the basis for the implica­
tions and future research agenda discussed in the next section.

VI. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
Discussion
This discussion is organized around the two research questions that guided the review. RQ1 asked what intellectual 
structure, application domains, and theory-context-method patterns characterize ML research in accounting. RQ2 
asked how accounting antecedents shape ML design and validation decisions and how these decisions condition 
reported outcomes and future research gaps. We answer RQ1 by showing that the literature is concentrated around 
two application cores—distress/bankruptcy prediction and reporting quality/fraud/misstatement detection—with audit 
analytics and textual analysis connecting these domains. We answer RQ2 by showing that the main limitation is not 
the absence of ML applications, but the incomplete alignment between accounting conditions and ML evaluation, vali­
dation, and governance choices.
The ADO synthesis further clarifies why ML is adopted (antecedents), how it is designed and validated (decisions), 
and what it delivers (outcomes). The most frequently observed antecedents relate to (1) increased assurance and compli­
ance pressure, (2) growth in high-dimensional and unstructured information (e.g., narratives, audit reports, system 
traces), and (3) the diffusion of digital process infrastructures enabling continuous monitoring. Design choices reflect 
recurring tradeoffs between predictive power and auditability: many studies benchmark flexible learners (ensembles, 
neural architectures, NLP pipelines) against interpretable baselines, but validation and transparency practices remain 
uneven across the corpus (e.g., temporal robustness, leakage controls, and documentation of preprocessing and thresh­
olds). Outcomes converge on incremental predictive performance and broader testing scope (often framed as fuller- 
population analytics), but reported benefits are conditional on disciplined validation, reproducible pipelines, and 
governance-aligned deployment assumptions.
Two conceptual points follow. First, our review treats ML as the focal construct (a data-driven subset of AI) and 
includes studies only when the analytical contribution relies on learning-based models (supervised/unsupervised/deep 
learning, ensembles, and related pipelines). This boundary is important because broad “AI/digitalization” framing can 
blur the distinction between what is being reviewed and what is inferred from the evidence base. Second, theoretical 
lenses are present but not dominant: many papers use theory primarily to motivate the substantive setting (e.g., fraud,

Implications for Research
Responding to RQ2, the ADO synthesis shows that the largest gaps occur when accounting antecedents and ML 
design choices are misaligned. Methodologically, future work would benefit from (1) temporally robust validation as a 
norm in settings with regime shifts, (2) explicit treatment of cost asymmetries and thresholding aligned with audit/plan­
ning objectives, and (3) reporting standards that make pipelines reproducible (feature construction, preprocessing, 
leakage safeguards, stability checks). Conceptually, research can tighten alignment between theory and ML practice by 
linking adoption and use to accountability, decision usefulness, and institutional constraints, and by examining how 
ML outputs are incorporated into human judgment and review routines (rather than treating explainability as an 
add-on) (Table 7). These research implications follow directly from the ADO-TCM evidence: stronger ML accounting 
research will require not only more sophisticated models but also clearer theorization of why a given model, validation 
design, or explanation format is appropriate for a specific accounting task.
Practical Implications
Because our evidence is drawn from published studies (not from field deployments), implications for practice 
should be interpreted as design considerations rather than prescriptive policy guidance. In audit and control contexts, 
the literature most consistently supports three implementable emphases: (1) validation designs that reflect the time 
structure of accounting settings, (2) documentation and provenance practices that support reviewability, and (3) expla­
nation interfaces that are evaluated on whether they improve reviewer decisions, not only on whether they can be gen­
erated. These points follow directly from recurrent boundary conditions reported across the corpus (performance 
claims weaken when temporal dependence, leakage controls, or documentation are underspecified). For practitioners, 
the practical message is that ML systems used in accounting and assurance should be assessed as decision-support 
infrastructure rather than solely as prediction engines. Their value depends on whether model outputs can be validated 
over time, traced back to reliable data and preprocessing choices, explained to reviewers, and incorporated into 
accountable professional judgment.
Limitations
This review has four limitations. First, WoSCC supports transparency and consistent metadata but omits relevant 
work indexed only in Scopus, Google Scholar, conference proceedings, practitioner outlets, or computer-science data­
bases. Second, topic-field searching can miss papers that use ML without foregrounding ML terms. Third, bibliometric 
thresholds and ADO-TCM coding involve judgment despite dual screening and reconciliation. Fourth, the review syn­
thesizes reported evidence and cannot establish the field effectiveness of ML tools without deployment evidence.
Conclusion
In conclusion, the literature on ML in accounting is expanding and increasingly clustered around distress predic­
tion and reporting integrity/fraud problems, with growing attention to unstructured disclosures and audit analytics. 
The ADO-TCM synthesis shows that ML adoption is motivated mainly by scalability and information complexity; 
design decisions hinge on the tradeoff between predictive lift and accountability-oriented constraints; and reported out­
comes emphasize improved screening and expanded coverage but are contingent on validation discipline and transpar­
ency. Future work can strengthen the field by aligning theoretical mechanisms with ML design choices, standardizing 
temporally robust and cost-aware evaluation, and producing evidence on how humans incorporate ML outputs into 
accounting judgments and assurance processes.
The contribution of this review is to connect these pieces in one cumulative framework. The bibliometric analysis 
maps the development of ML accounting research; ADO explains why ML is adopted, how it is designed, and the out­
comes claimed; and TCM shows how theory, context, and method shape the evidence base. This integrated view 
reframes future research away from asking only whether ML improves prediction and toward asking when ML
