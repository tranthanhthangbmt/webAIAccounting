5.1 SEGMENTING CUSTOMERS
Vegan-Always is a new restaurant opening in Phoenix. It wants to send out
coupons to customers who enjoy vegan food. Should it send these coupons to all
residents of Phoenix? While this can be done, not only would such extensive
coupon distribution become very expensive, but not everyone would be interested
in the restaurant’ s offerings. Since not all customers are alike or share the same
tastes, sending the same marketing message to everyone is not ef ficient or
profitable. The best strategy for V egan-Always is to divide the entire population of
Phoenix in such a way that it can send the coupons only to those who are likely to
visit the restaurant. One way the restaurant can do this is by using
psychographics or lifestyle factors. People who are more environmentally
conscious have turned to veganism and might be more interested in such a
restaurant.
Similarly , demographics can be another way to find groups of customers who
would be relevant for the restaurant. Such a process of dividing the market into
groups of customers with similar characteristics and selecting the most
appropriate group(s) for the business to target is called market segmentation.
One way to think of segmentation is as a comprise between mass marketing
(which assumes that all customers are the same) and individual-level
customization (which assumes that all customers are unique). Instead of targeting
each customer , segmentation allows a business to target groups of customers
who are similar in the way they respond to the product. In today’ s data-driven
markets, it is now possible to segment at a deeper level; hence, microsegmenting
is becoming popular . For example, based on past buying behavior and similarity
to other customers, Amazon has the ability to create microsegments and
recommend very specific products to each of its customers.
The first step in market segmentation is to know why customers are buying a
product. What benefits is the product of fering that a customer may be interested
in? The business needs to identify all customer needs that a product or service
can fulfill. For instance, an automobile can be purchased to satisfy transportation
needs or status needs. Knowing exactly which need is being satisfied for which
group of customers can help in more accurate messaging and targeting.
Identifying product-related needs can run the gamut from using traditional
methods such as focus groups and surveys to the recent natural language
processing (NLP) methods that can be used to mine customer opinions and
reviews. Customer needs may not be restricted to just product features but may
also include the company reputation. In the 1990s, Nike lost sales because of
news that it was using child labor in its manufacturing units. The company had to
change its working conditions because customers also wanted Nike to use the
right practices. Once customers’  needs are identified, customers can be
categorized into groups with similar needs that form the dif ferent segments  for
that business. Developing a market with an imaginary buyer whose
characteristics are inaccurate is a recipe for failure. Many products have failed not
because they were bad but because they were marketed to the wrong set of
customers.
After businesses have grouped customers with similar characteristics in terms of
how they respond to product benefits, they can then decide which of these
customer groups are most profitable to target. The process of determining which
of the segments of fer good market opportunities is called targeting . Finally , when
businesses have segmented customers and decided which of these segments to
target, they need to determine how they want to position their products in the
minds of their customers. Positioning  can be described as instilling a meaning in
customers’  minds that is unique to that product. W e will first understand
segmentation in detail before moving on to targeting and positioning.
Segmentation, targeting, and positioning are generally known as the STPs of
marketing  since these three steps tend to be conducted together .
An initial step in segmentation is to group customers by similarities in their
characteristics. Customers may seek similar benefits from a product because they
live in the same region. For instance, customers who share geographic similarity
may face the same weather conditions; people living in colder climes may need
heavier jackets that can provide warmth compared to those living in warmer
places where such jackets may not be necessary .
We next discuss the dif ferent basis on which customers could be grouped
together in terms of similar characteristics.
5.5.1 Basis of Segmentation
Geographic . Customers can be segmented based on where they live. For
instance, an insurance company might of fer dif ferent types of insurance based on
whether customers are located in regions prone to tornadoes, floods, or
earthquakes.
Demographic.  A very common way to segment customers is by demographic
features such as age, education, gender , income, and occupation. For example,
businesses may target millennials dif ferently from baby boomers. Baby boomers
account for a large share of the market currently because they control a major
portion of the disposable income in the United States, which makes them a very
attractive segment of the market. Hence, it is important that a business planning
to target baby boomers understand the features of this segment. Are they health
conscious? Are they tech savvy? W ould they be willing to buy online? Are they
philanthropic compared to other segments? Businesses also need to keep in
mind that features defining a group change with time. W omen now purchase
repair tools as often as men.
Psychological.  Customers can be segmented based on their dif ferent
psychological factors, such as motivation, personality , and perceptions.
Customers can purchase a brand to satisfy a prestige motive or for a functional
motive. Rolex and Timex both manufacture watches, but people might be buying
them for dif ferent motives. Personality can also be used to segment customers.
Companies could consider the Big 5 personality traits1 such as whether an
individual is extroverted, disinhibited, neurotic, conscientious, or open to
experience. Or a business could focus on personality traits that have been shown
to affect marketplace decisions, such as impulsivity , loyalty , frugality ,
innovativeness, variety seeking, and risk aversion. Despite psychological factors
being very informative of how customers may behave in the marketplace, it is
difficult to obtain this information. Until recently , one way has been through the
use of surveys, but this cannot be done for all customers and may be
inconvenient and expensive. Some work has demonstrated that digital footprints
of customers can be used to predict their personality . Digital footprints can be in
the form of text, messages in online forums, number of likes, size of the networks
frequented, and so on. Knowing the personality of customers allows for deeper
customization of messages and recommendations targeting these individuals.
Sociocultural.  This could include stages in the life cycle, such as whether one is
single, married, or an empty nester , or social class, such as whether one belongs
to the upper , lower , middle, or working class. It also could include cultural values,
a very personal way that individuals define their core beliefs. Since cultural
segmentation maps onto core beliefs and values, it can help the business or
brand connect with a customer at a more meaningful level. In this age of
globalization, with products marketed across the world, being able to connect to
unique cultures is a challenge and yet a big asset if it can be done successfully .
Psychographic.  The psychographic basis of segmentation includes activities,
interests, and opinions of customers. Customers who like to vacation in
Yellowstone are likely to share similar interests and require dif ferent messaging
tactics than those who prefer a Disneyland vacation. Customers with dif ferent
political opinions (e.g., conservative versus liberal) may respond to dif ferent
marketing strategies. Those who are risk averse may not be open to buying a
product that is positioned as adventurous.
Benefit Sought.  What kind of benefits are people likely to look for when buying a
product? Let’ s take the example of running shoes. They could provide more than
one benefit, such as durability , comfort, speed, and appearance. They could even
be used for some form of social signaling to inform others that one is a runner .
Multivitamins are another example of a product that could convey dif ferent
benefits for dif ferent segments of customers—such as strength, prevention of
illness, and boosting of immunity .
Usage V olume.  Customers can be segmented based on how often they buy a
product and how often they use it (i.e., whether they are heavy , medium, or light
users). It is possible that heavy users form a smaller percentage of the total users
of a product but may actually buy a substantial share of the product. Hence, they
are an important segment to focus on.
Product Attitude.  Having information about the type of attitude customers hold
about a brand can help determine how they will react to a new or modified of fering
by the brand. Some customers may have extremely favorable or extremely
unfavorable brand attitudes. For example, some PC users would not think of
using a Mac, and vice versa. Such potentially nonconvertible customer segments
may not be worth targeting.
5.2 TARGETING POTENTIAL CUSTOMERS
Segmentation reveals specific market opportunities. Once customers have been
segmented based on how they respond to product features, the business needs
to evaluate the customer segments and decide which to target. It rarely happens
that a product has mass appeal. Therefore, finding the segments that are likely to
purchase the product is important. W e can understand the process of selecting
viable segments to target through the following questions that a business needs
to ask.
Are there enough segments in the market worth targeting? Having a product
that is liked by a very small group of customers indicates that the business
may have a niche product, which may not be profitable on a broader level.
Is the segment stable, and will it remain stable for some time? It’ s not ideal if
the target segments are comprised of people who are interested in fads.
Growing and stable segments are more attractive and more likely to be
profitable in the long term.
Are the segments accessible? For instance, a large segment of customers
may be interested in the product but may be in a place where it cannot be
accessed easily .
How well does this product compare with the competition in the chosen target
segments? If the product is not the innovator or the first entrant in the market,
chances are there are one or more businesses already catering to the
segments that the business finds attractive. The business needs to find out
how their product compares to existing products. If the business already has
very strong competitors catering to a segment and the segment is also very
brand loyal, it may be very hard to break into such a market.
5.3 POSITIONING THE PRODUCT IN
CUSTOMERS’ MINDS
Positioning is defined as an ef fort by a business to identify a unique selling
proposition that allows its product to occupy a clear , distinctive, and attractive
position in the minds of target customers relative to competing products in the
market. Successful positioning includes communicating the benefits of the
product as well as communicating a unique selling proposition.
Many examples exist of businesses and their brands occupying a unique position
in the minds of their customers. Some are so successful that the brands have
become synonymous with the product category . For instance, one says Kleenex
to mean facial tissue, FedEx to mean shipping, or Xerox to mean photocopying.
Oral-B realized that if it wanted customers to change their toothbrushes rather
than keep using worn-out ones, the company needed to communicate this to the
customer . The company introduced a patented dye on the brush head that fades
with usage and indicates that it is time to get a new toothbrush. Lay’ s tries to
position itself on taste through its slogan of Betcha can’t eat just one , and
Volkswagen positions itself by focusing on driving pleasure with its tagline Drivers
wanted .
A brand can position itself based on a specific product attribute, or it can position
itself in comparison to a competitor ’s offering. Some automobile brands position
their vehicles using product attributes; for example, Honda and Toyota focus on
durability , reliability , and functional benefits while V olvo focuses on safety and
Volkswagen focuses on drivability . Other automobile brands position themselves
versus other brands, such as Hyundai against Toyota, to show that they of fer the
same or greater benefits.
We next discuss how segmentation has changed in a data-rich marketplace.
5.4 DATA-DRIVEN SEGMENTATION
As mentioned earlier , the higher volume of data available to businesses makes
the following types of segmentation feasible: segmentation based on behavioral
data, dynamic segmentation, and microsegmentation.
5.4.1 Behavioral Segmentation
In the world of Big Data, an abundance of information, including click-through and
search data, is available about people’ s behavior: which products they are buying,
where they are buying these products, how much they are paying, whether a
product is more likely to sell in specific packaging, and so on. Behavioral data can
be used to extrapolate demographic and psychographic information. Many times
it is dif ficult to find out the specific personality traits of consumers that result in
their purchasing a brand (e.g., is a customer more or less frugal?). However ,
behavioral data, even from social media, can be used to predict the general
personality of customers. Moreover , with the proliferation of online retailers, it has
become easy to conduct ongoing studies to figure out customer preferences.
Instead of trying to determine the segment of customers that is most likely to
purchase a brand by a study based on attitudes (e.g., how much do customers
like this brand?), a product can be introduced via small samples, and actual
purchase behavior can be monitored. Also, based on sales data, products can be
modified in real time based on which features customers most prefer . Hence,
data-driven markets allow a feedback loop by which businesses can observe
actual customer purchases and fine-tune market segments.
5.4.2 Dynamic Segmentation
Another outcome of rich data about customers allows a business to keep
updating its customer segments. Rather than segmenting all customers once and
assuming that their tastes are not going to change, dynamic segmentation
assumes that tastes will change and keeps updating what of fering would be best
preferred by a specific customer segment. Many aspects in an individual’ s life can
change. For example, a chocolate manufacturer cannot assume that just because
a customer has purchased dark chocolate in the past they will continue to do so.
Their personal, family , or health needs could change, causing them to move to
buying sugar-free or nut-free chocolates. Dynamic segmentation takes into
account the possibility that customer tastes change and, hence, that the segment
they belong to is not static. Again, it is important that businesses hone their ability
to sift through the wealth of information available to them and use the most recent
information, whether behavioral or otherwise, to segment customers.
5.4.3 Microsegments
As mentioned earlier , it has now become possible to cater to very small segments
of customers by understanding their preferences in depth and customizing
products accordingly . This also leads to hyperpersonalization, where each of fering
uniquely targets a specific customer based on both demographic and behavioral
data gathered about that customer . For instance, insurance companies can
augment existing data such as age, gender , and current product portfolio with
online behavior such as time spent on their website to better segment customers
and of fer the customer a very customized product.
As important as data are for segmentation, the algorithm chosen to perform the
segmentation is equally important. Many times we just have dif ferent types of data
about customers without information about a business outcome such as sales or
whether or not such a customer is loyal. That is, the data tend to be unlabeled.
Because we do not have any outcome labels, we do not have the ability to
compare model performances. The most common task of unsupervised learning
is exploratory analysis, which uses visualization methods (the simplest being
clustering) to examine the data and gather insights.
Let’s go over what algorithms are commonly used for segmentation.
5.5 CLUSTERING ALGORITHMS FOR
SEGMENTATION
Clustering is frequently used to segment customers into groups with similar need
sets. For instance, if we want to find out which of our customers are most likely to
recommend our product or our business, we can cluster them by similarity of
attributes and try to gather insights from the clusters formed. Clustering is the
process of grouping together data points that share some similarity , such that data
points within a cluster are more similar to one another as well as more dissimilar
to data points in a dif ferent cluster . Hence, clustering does exploratory data
analysis and is considered an unsupervised algorithm because it explores data
patterns rather than confirms a specific relationship. Clustering algorithms are
used when the data lacks labels.
Several clustering algorithms exist, such as k-means, k-nearest neighbors,
hierarchical and Gaussian mixture models, and so on; there are several types and
subtypes of clustering algorithms as depicted in Figure 5.1 . Clustering mainly
occurs either by partitioning a large set of observations into smaller sets or by
creating a treelike structure to depict the relationship between observations. In
clustering, the process of dividing is called partition clustering, and k-means, k-
nearest neighbors, and mixture models fall into this category . Clustering by
creating a treelike structure, also called a dendrogram, involves relating each
observation to another observation. Such hierarchical clustering can build up from
one observation, find the nearest next observation, and so on to create a tree
structure (agglomerative clustering), or it can begin by considering all
observations to be in one cluster and then break down the cluster using the
distance metric (divisive clustering).

5.5.1 The Relationship Between Distance and
Similarity
The k-means algorithm is based on the intuitive notion that similar observations
are likely to be clustered together . The distance between observations is a way to
measure similarity: Less distance between observations implies that the
observations are likely to be similar , and more distance indicates dissimilar
observations. Thus, if the distance between two observations is less, it implies
that the observations are similar and can be clustered together within the same
cluster . If the distance between two observations is large, it implies that they are
dissimilar and probably cannot be clustered together .
We next discuss how distance is used as the metric to measure similarity of the
observations.
Let’s understand how distance is calculated between two observations. Assume
that the two observations are from two customers and that we have information
about two variables— age and income. W e can use these two variables to assess
the level of similarity between these two customers. If we call the two customers A
and B, then the two variables can be depicted as follows for customer A:
xA1=age,xA2=income
And for customer B:
xB1=age,xB2=income
The distance between two customers ( A→B) is calculated using the following
formula, which is also called the Euclidean distance formula.
distanceA→B=√(xA1−xB1)2+(xA2−xB2)2
We use the two dif ferent attributes x1 and x2 for both A and B to find the distance
between A and B. If we had information on a third customer , let’s say C, then we
could determine distance measures between customers A and B, B and C, and A
and C. Using this distance, we could state whether A was more similar to B or to
C since less distance indicates more similarity . Clustering algorithms utilize
distance as a way to group observations together . Observations that are more
similar are clustered together but separately from observations that are less
similar . We next discuss the most commonly used clustering algorithm, k-means.
5.5.2 The k-Means Algorithm
For any clustering algorithm, the goal is to find groups of similar observations
among all the observations in the given dataset. To accomplish this goal, the k-
means algorithm uses Euclidean distance to find out which observations are more
versus less similar . But this would become a very inef ficient task if we needed to
find distance estimates between each observation and every other observation.
Therefore, the first step in a k-means algorithm is to decide how many clusters
we, the user , want the algorithm to output. This is an issue with many clustering
algorithms, where the number of clusters has to be provided by the user , and the
algorithm accordingly clusters observations into the specified number of groups.
For instance, if we ask the algorithm to cluster 100 observations into 5 groups, it
will go ahead and do so, and if we ask it to cluster the same 100 observations into
20 groups, it will do that also. W e will discuss the issue of choosing the
appropriate number of clusters later .
We illustrate the steps of the k-means algorithm as follows:
1. Randomly select k points as initial centroids
2. repeat
3. Assign data points to the closest centroid
4. Recalculate the centroid of each cluster
5. until  Centroids do not change
Now let’ s visually understand these steps using a figure. Figure 5.2 (a) shows
observations, with each point representing a customer ’s age (x-axis) and income
(y-axis). W e set k=3, which means we want the algorithm to assign
observations to one of the three clusters. Once we provide the number of
clusters, the k-means algorithm randomly selects k locations as center points or
centroids . Figure 5.2 (b) shows these randomly selected centroids. The distance
from each observation to each of the k centroids is measured, and then each
observation is assigned to the centroid closest to it. At the end of the first iteration,
each observation has been assigned to one of the k clusters. The color of the
points in Figure 5.2 (b) indicates their cluster membership.
Description
Figure 5.2  k-Means
The k-means algorithm next finds the new centroids of the newly formed k
clusters. Recall that a centroid is nothing but the central point of a cluster . Since
the initial centroids were randomly chosen, we can see that the location of the
new centroid changes (see Figure 5.3 (a)). Again, the distance of each
observation to these new k centroids is calculated, and the observations are
reassigned to the centroid closest to it.
Description
Figure 5.3  k-Means After Iteration
In this next iteration, k clusters are again formed. In the subsequent iteration,
centroids are calculated for these k clusters, and the process is repeated till the
centroids do not change (see Figure 5.3 (b)). The stability of the centroids
indicates that this is the optimal allocation of observations to k clusters. If we ask
for a dif ferent number of clusters, then the k-means algorithm will go through the
same process until it finds the optimal allocation of observations to the number of
clusters that we have specified.
5.5.3 The k-Medoid Algorithm
One of the issues with k-means is that outliers can influence the clusters that are
generated. An improvement over the k-means algorithm is k-medoid, which
modifies k-means to take care of this issue. While k-means randomly chooses
centroids of various clusters, k-medoid first chooses data points as centers of
clusters. Then instead of using Euclidean distance, it uses Manhattan distance to
find the closest cluster center to a point. Manhattan distance is calculated by
measuring the absolute distance between any two points and uses the following
formula:
dmanhattan=n

i=1|xi−yi|
By using Manhattan distance to calculate the proximity of a point to a cluster
center , k-medoid is less influenced by outliers and noise in the data.
An example of using k-means and k-medoid to segment customers will help us
understand how the algorithm can be used for segmentation.
5.5.4 Using k-Means to Segment Customers
We pick an example with just two variables because it is easy to visualize,
although k-means can be used for a large number of variables. (The data for this
example are hypothetical.) A common query of most retail stores is whether they
should target customers who spend more or customers who have been their
patrons for longer (i.e., is amount of money spent or duration more important?).
Therefore, we have two input variables: amount spent  and duration of patronage .
We first scale the data by subtracting the means and dividing by the standard
deviation (details of scaling and standardization are provided in the Technical
Appendix). Scaling the data prevents data distortions that would occur in
clustering if the input variables are on dif ferent scales; the variable with a bigger
unit would be overrepresented.
Then we tell the k-means algorithm how many clusters we want it to output. This
is an important consideration for most clustering algorithms, which require that the
user specify the number of clusters into which the observations need to be
segmented. Here we ask it to output three clusters. Figure 5.4  illustrates the three
clusters.

We can then interpret that members of cluster 1 have an average patronage

2 have a similar patronage length to those in cluster 1 (−1.006 months) but have

the same duration but not spend as much. W ould some form of incentive such as
coupons for loyal customers motivate them to spend more?
An important question (some even call it a challenge) in any clustering analysis is
knowing the best number of clusters to choose. Since we must specify the
number of clusters for the algorithm, how do we know whether three or seven will
be the best number of clusters? Many times it depends on the question we are
trying to answer . Or we can use past information that shows the number of
clusters obtained when we used similar data. A silhouette  helps us answer the
question of choosing the optimal number of clusters. A silhouette analysis is
based on the simple intuition that if an observation is in a cluster , then that
observation should be closer to those in the same cluster than to those in other
clusters. A silhouette coef ficient for each observation is calculated and varies
between −1 and +1, with +1 indicating the best and −1 being the worst allocation
to a cluster . The average silhouette coef ficient for all observations is known as the
average silhouette width . An average silhouette width near 1 indicates a tight
cluster , with observations in the cluster close together rather than spread all over .
For instance, if a four-cluster solution gives a silhouette value of 0.7, and a seven-
cluster solution gives a silhouette value of 0.3, it means that the four-cluster
solution is better .

Therefore, we can conclude our clustering analysis with the following insights.
When we want to perform segmentation, we can use a clustering algorithm such
k-means or k-medoid. W e use clustering because our data are unlabeled (i.e.,
they do not have an outcome label). In the example, we find that when amount
spent and patronage duration are used and we ask for three clusters, we can
obtain the average characteristics of customers in the three clusters. The
differences between these customers inform us how the business can present
effective product of ferings to customers in these three segments.
